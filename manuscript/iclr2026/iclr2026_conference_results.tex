\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

% Paragraph purpose: Map the four subsections onto findings F1-F4 and say once how the two controls are read.
This section reports four findings. Section~\ref{sec:results-data-cost} shows that all four algorithms can be learned from traces, at very different data costs. Section~\ref{sec:results-modality} shows that the learned execution relies on the text of each observation, Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness} that it keeps working under task variations, and Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer} that BFWS traces bring a small, not statistically significant gain in logical reasoning. Two controls appear throughout. Random-valid emits a uniformly drawn valid operation at each decision, and the exact reference runs the declared algorithm itself. Both read the runtime's list of valid operations, so we treat them as oracle-assisted bounds on what valid execution reaches, not as learned abilities (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

\subsection{All Four Algorithms Are Learnable, at Different Data Costs}
\label{sec:results-data-cost}

% Paragraph purpose: State the main-grid result: at 512 records the two additive variants are learned and BFS/BFWS are not, with the base failing on format.
With 512 training records per adapter, fine-tuning teaches the two additive variants but not BFS or BFWS (Table~\ref{tab:results-main}, top). Each record is one search decision taken from a replay-verified reference trace. The greedy best-first and weighted-A* adapters solve 125/144 task-observation entries, whereas the pretrained base solves none and mostly fails its first decision on output format (Table~\ref{tab:appendix-rejection}). The BFS and BFWS adapters solve 0/24 tasks under every observation.

% Paragraph purpose: Say once, plainly, that random-valid's full additive score is expected behaviour of the control, with the appendix pointer.
Random-valid solves every additive task, which is the expected behavior of this control. In the additive cells the runtime supplies the grounded candidates and orders the priority queue, so on these tasks any valid emission reproduces the reference's search (Appendix~\ref{sec:results-identity}). The additive result therefore shows that the adapters learned to execute the algorithm's steps validly, and random-valid marks the ceiling that valid execution reaches.

% Paragraph purpose: Report the large-corpus development runs in which BFS and BFWS are learned, and state that they are not a matched-size comparison.
BFS and BFWS become learnable when the corpus grows by more than an order of magnitude, although we tested no corpus size between 512 and about 11,900 records, so the minimum that suffices is unknown (Table~\ref{tab:results-main}, bottom). Text BFS adapters trained on 12,994 records for three epochs solve every task of a 15-task development panel at each of five training seeds, and random-valid also solves every task there. A text BFWS adapter trained on 47,780 records for two epochs reaches 0.933 on another 15-task panel, against 0.467 for random-valid, and the paired bootstrap 95\% lower bound on its gain is 0.2. A visual run on 43,876 records across the four algorithms, including 11,911 for BFS and 14,225 for BFWS, solves 75/75 BFS episodes (random-valid 53/75) and 50/75 BFWS episodes (random-valid 39/75), and both additive adapters solve every episode, as random-valid does. These runs use their own development panels and schedules, so they are not a matched-size comparison with the main grid. They establish that BFS and BFWS can be learned from traces, and that 512 records are not enough under our recipe.

% Paragraph purpose: Offer one tentative explanation of why BFS and BFWS cost more, grounded in what a valid step must contain (Section 3), and show that the 512-record failures differ by algorithm in the way the explanation expects.
One explanation, which we have not tested, is that BFS and BFWS steps each ask more of the policy than an additive step does, in different ways. The runtime flags each candidate as unvisited or duplicate and computes the BFWS novelty, goal count, and implied frontier position. A BFS step must still pick an unvisited candidate and emit first-in, first-out bookkeeping, meaning the candidate's queue position and whether the step removes the head. A BFWS step must follow a larger operation and observation format, with object arguments written as indices and more fields to copy, including the frontier position that the runtime's priority implies. An additive step only names the expanded state and one listed candidate, with no position or heuristic value (Section~\ref{sec:policy}). The 512-record failures split the same way. Of the 72 BFS failures, 68 are well-formed steps that name a listed successor but misstate the queue bookkeeping, and the other 4 retire the frontier too early. Every BFWS failure is a first operation that does not follow the BFWS operation format (Figure~\ref{fig:qual-trace-failures}, Appendix~\ref{app:results-failures}).

\subsection{Text Carries the Learned Execution}
\label{sec:results-modality}

% Paragraph purpose: Report the corruption result and why the claim rests on shuffling rather than masking.
The trained controllers depend on the text of their observations and hardly on the images. We corrupted the observations of the frozen additive adapters at test time, without retraining, on 9 tasks under both additive algorithms (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c, Table~\ref{tab:results-corruption}). Shuffling the text while leaving its operations copyable changes success by $-0.944$ $[-1.000, -0.833]$ in the text arm and by $-0.722$ in the multimodal arm. Blanking or degrading the images changes success by at most $-0.111$, and every visual interval includes zero. Masking the text also collapses success, but it does so by breaking the output format, since every masked episode emits only invalid operations. The channel claim therefore rests on shuffling.

% Paragraph purpose: Carry boundary 4: candidates are text in every observation, image-only arms still solve with only the textual menu, and interpret for planning readers.
This finding has a specific scope. In all three observation types of the main grid, the runtime supplies the grounded candidate list and the search memory as text, and only the state and goal become images. Image-only arms that drop every textual aid except the task interface and this candidate menu still solve 18/18 tasks, and blanking every image of the arm that also sees rendered history leaves its success unchanged (Appendix~\ref{app:results-native}). For planning, the result means that the trained controllers read the textual serialization of the search, so rendering the state does not yet add information that the model uses. It does not show that images are useless for planning in principle.

\subsection{Robustness to Task Variations}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

% Paragraph purpose: Report robustness v2 on the additive adapters, per family, with saturated controls and the scope boundary.
The learned additive search keeps working when tasks are varied. We evaluated the six main-grid additive adapters, two algorithms under three observations, on 25 derived tasks drawn five each from five variation families and frozen before execution. They solve 128/150 episodes, while the pretrained base solves 0/150 (Appendix~\ref{app:results-secondary}). By family, they solve 29/30 with renamed objects, 27/30 with compressed names, 27/30 with restyled renderings, and 26/30 on scaled-up instances. Tasks that start from a shifted initial state are the hardest, at 19/30. Random-valid (750/750) and the exact reference (150/150) saturate, so these counts show that learned execution survives the variations, not that it exceeds the controls. Each family holds five tasks, so per-family counts are descriptive. We tested only the two additive adapters on variants, not BFS or BFWS.

% Paragraph purpose: Juxtapose with plan-trained models reported to degrade under anonymization, with boundary 2 wording.
These variations resemble those under which a plan-trained model has been reported to degrade. \citet{belcamino2026generalization} fine-tune a 1.7B-parameter language model on domain-problem-plan tuples and report significant drops under instance-wise symbol anonymization and compact plan serialization. The two setups differ. Their model writes a whole plan, while ours emits one search step at a time from grounded candidates that the runtime supplies. We ran no plan-only baseline, and our controls saturate, so the two results sit side by side and cannot be ranked against each other.

\subsection{Transfer Beyond Planning}
\label{sec:results-transfer}

% Paragraph purpose: Report the BFWS FOLIO gain under all three observations, the math/code ranges, and the Holm result.
Training on BFWS traces raises logical-reasoning accuracy under all three observations, a consistent but not statistically significant gain. We evaluated all 12 main-grid adapters zero-shot on frozen subsets of FOLIO \citep{han2022folio} (200 items), GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} (200), and HumanEval \citep{chen2021humaneval} (164). On FOLIO the BFWS adapters score 0.660, 0.645, and 0.645 (text, visual, multimodal) against 0.600 for the base, while the other adapters score between 0.575 and 0.605. GSM8K accuracy stays between 0.945 and 0.965 for every adapter (base 0.955), and HumanEval between 0.756 and 0.817 (base 0.780). No adapter-versus-base comparison survives Holm correction over all 36 comparisons \citep{holm1979simple}. The smallest raw McNemar $p$ \citep{mcnemar1947note} is 0.023, for the text BFWS adapter on FOLIO, and the minimum adjusted $p$ is 0.82 (Table~\ref{tab:results-transfer}, Appendix~\ref{app:results-secondary}). A leakage screen finds no content overlap between the benchmarks and the planning corpus.
