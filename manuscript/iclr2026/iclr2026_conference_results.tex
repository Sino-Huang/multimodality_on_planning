\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\label{sec:discussion}

% Paragraph purpose: Map the three subsections onto the Introduction's findings and contributions (what each level costs; what the learned search carries), and restate the two findings that run against expectation.
A VLM learns both levels of search from traces, and the two levels differ in what they cost. Sections~\ref{sec:results-data-cost} and~\ref{sec:results-search-control} report what each level costs, which is our second contribution, and Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer} reports what the learned search carries beyond planning, our third. Two results run against a natural expectation: the simplest algorithm, blind BFS, needs over an order of magnitude more data to execute than a small training set provides, whereas the choice that a heuristic search computes from an estimate is learned from images of the open nodes alone. Each subsection reports its result and then discusses what it shows.
% Evidence: see the evidence comments of each subsection below.

\subsection{Executing BFS and BFWS Is Learnable, but Only with Far More Data}
\label{sec:results-data-cost}

% Paragraph purpose: The small set teaches neither algorithm in any observation type; state how each algorithm fails and what random-valid shows about the tasks.
A small training set does not teach the model to execute BFS or BFWS in any observation type (Table~\ref{tab:results-main}, top). Trained on 512 reference operations for one epoch, the adapters solve none of the 24 tasks under text, visual or multimodal observations, and every episode ends on an invalid operation. The two algorithms fail in different ways (Figure~\ref{fig:qual-trace-failures} in Appendix~\ref{app:qualitative}). BFS adapters write well-formed steps that name an unvisited successor but give the wrong head-removal flag $r$ or queue position $p$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:operation}). BFWS adapters never write a well-formed operation, so each episode ends at its opening decision. Random-valid solves 15 of the 24 BFS tasks and 17 of the 24 BFWS tasks, so a valid but random execution already reaches most goals within the budget, and the adapters fall short of it only through invalid operations.

% Paragraph purpose: Report the larger development runs by observation type with random-valid alongside, saying which runs beat the control and which does not.
With over an order of magnitude more data, the model executes both algorithms from text and from images (Table~\ref{tab:results-main}, bottom). From text, BFS trained on 12,994 operations solves every task of its panel at each of five training seeds, and BFWS trained on 47,780 solves 14 of 15 tasks. From images, which in these runs keep object names in some domains (Section~\ref{sec:design}), BFS trained on 11,911 operations solves all 75 episodes (15 tasks, five rollout seeds each), and BFWS trained on 14,225 solves 50 of 75. Three of the four runs exceed random-valid, text BFWS by 0.467 with a paired bootstrap 95\% lower bound of 0.2. The pretrained base solves no task in any run.

% Paragraph purpose: State the unmatched boundary of the data-cost comparison and what it leaves open.
These runs show that enough data suffices but do not measure how much is needed. Each ran for two or three epochs on its own 15-task panel, so it differs from the small set in tasks, schedule and panel, and we trained no BFS or BFWS set between 512 and 11,911 operations. The smallest sufficient size, and whether it differs between BFS and BFWS, remain open.
% Evidence: epochs and panels as in the paragraph above; small set one epoch on 24 tasks (Section 4). No BFS/BFWS training size between 512 and 11,911 exists in the evidence repo (small set 512; larger runs 11,911 / 12,994 / 14,225 / 47,780).

% Paragraph purpose: Discussion. More on-policy correction and a second model do not rescue the small set; state only what the two controls show, without a causal reading of the failure modes.
Neither on-policy correction nor a different model rescues the small set. Two rounds of DAgger \citep{ross2011dagger}, which add the model's own mistakes with the reference's corrections to the training set, lift small-set BFS from 0 to only 1 of its 72 evaluation episodes, the same as continuing supervised training on the same number of records, while random-valid solves 45. Trained on the same small set, InternVL3.5-8B \citep{wang2025internvl35} also solves none of 36 BFS episodes, so the small-set failure is specific to neither the training recipe nor the backbone.

% Evidence (second model): outputs/expanded-study/v1/second-backbone-v2/evaluation/analysis.json (protocol expanded-second-backbone-v2, BFS, 12 tasks x 3 observation types, process_sft 0/36, base 0/36; 512 records, seed 17 per synthesis-v1/README.md L125).
% Removed 2026-09-26 (author request): the causal reading "points to the amount of data as the bottleneck" and the untested failure-mode interpretation (bookkeeping vs ordering rule). The paragraph now reports only what DAgger and the second backbone show.

% Paragraph purpose: Discussion. Why the runtime supplies successors: when the model generates successor states itself, BFS search collapses; ties back to the design choice in Section 3 and to operational reasoning.
Supplying the successors is what keeps these results about search. In a separate BFS experiment, adapters trained to generate each successor state themselves were exactly right on 65 of 109 predictions, and because one wrong state ends the episode, the search reached the goal in 1 of 45 episodes, against 45 of 45 when the runtime supplies the successors. Generating successors is the operational reasoning that \citet{huang2026twodistinct} separate from reasoning about the structure of the search, and it would dominate any measurement of search if the model had to do both.

\subsection{Choosing the Next Node Is Learnable from Images Alone}
\label{sec:results-search-control}

% Paragraph purpose: Headline node-choice result on both held-out panels against random choice and the reference, with the seed range.
Trained on reference choices and shown only images of the open nodes, the VLM chooses which node GBFS and WA* expand next far better than random choice on held-out tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:node-choice}a--c). Within twice the reference's number of expansions, it solves 0.621 of the tasks on held-out 1 and 0.278 on held-out 2, averaged over three training seeds whose values span 0.545 to 0.682 and 0.208 to 0.333. Random valid choices solve 0.036 and 0.017, and the reference solves every task.
% Evidence: outputs/choice-frontier/v4/panels/metrics/analysis.json arms.p2 (held-out 1, 11 tasks x GBFS/WA*) .solved_at_2: learned_adapter_s17 0.636, s29 0.545, s71 0.682 (mean 0.621), random_valid 0.036, exact_reference 1;
% arms.p2u (held-out 2, 12 tasks x GBFS/WA*): learned s17 0.333, s29 0.208, s71 0.292 (mean 0.278), random_valid 0.017, exact_reference 1. Seed means derived (1.864/3, 0.833/3).

% Paragraph purpose: Place the learned choices on the validated area measure: contrast with random, position relative to the exact-epsilon ladder, and the zero-shot base at random level.
An area measure places the learned choices well above random choice and well below the reference. This measure, M1, is the area under the solved fraction as the budget grows from one to two times the reference's expansions, and it orders a ladder of privileged selectors, the reference with a fraction $\epsilon$ of its choices made at random, as expected on both panels (Appendix~\ref{sec:results-choice-frontier}). The three-seed mean M1 exceeds that of random choice by $D_3=+0.416$ $[+0.250, +0.591]$ on held-out 1 and $+0.149$ $[+0.030, +0.283]$ on held-out 2 (95\% task-cluster bootstrap). On held-out 1 it also exceeds the $\epsilon=0.75$ rung, by $+0.227$ $[+0.053, +0.403]$. It is not separated from that rung on held-out 2, nor over 35 tasks that pool both panels with the third 12-task panel ($+0.058$ $[-0.040, +0.156]$). The same VLM without the adapter, told the output format and asked for the open node that looks closest to the goal, makes only valid choices yet stays at the level of random choice ($D_3=+0.005$ on the third panel and $+0.001$ on held-out 1), so the gain comes from training on the traces.

% Paragraph purpose: Discussion. What the learned choices prefer: low-h_add states far more often than uniform, yet the exact reference choice only modestly above chance; the model learned a coarse preference, not the ordering.
The learned choices follow the heuristic coarsely rather than exactly (Figure~\ref{fig:node-choice}d). On the held-out panels, 46.4\% of its choices fall in the lowest fifth of the menu ranked by $h_{\rm add}$, against 25.0\% expected from uniform choice on the same menus, yet it picks the reference node in only 24.1\% of decisions, against 12.4\% by chance. The model has thus learned from the images a preference for states that look close to the goal, although not the exact order that $h_{\rm add}$ imposes.
% Evidence: figures/fig_node_choice.py panel d (report fig_node_choice_report.md): held-out 1 + 2, seeds 17/29/71, GBFS and WA*, 3,373 non-singleton decisions (165 singleton menus excluded); lowest rank bin 1,566/3,373 = 46.4% vs uniform expectation 842.0/3,373 = 25.0%; heap-head agreement 813/3,373 = 24.1% vs chance 418.8/3,373 = 12.4%; h_add recovered from trusted admissions in the outputs/choice-frontier/v4/panels episodes (never shown to the model).

% Paragraph purpose: Discussion. A menu-position shortcut in the first, smaller attempt, and how position-balanced training removed it; a lesson for image-menu choice.
As with execution, a small training set was not enough, and the way it failed shows that the training data must hide the position of the answer. Trained on 512 choices per algorithm with each menu in one order, the model picked the last label in 54.5\% of its decisions, against 12.0\% for the reference choices it was trained on, and chose the reference node less often than chance (17.4\% against 26.9\%), so it behaved like a random picker. With 2,048 choices, each shown in two menu orders, the last-label rate fell to 20.5\% and agreement with the reference rose above chance (21.0\% against 12.5\%).

% Paragraph purpose: Discussion. Where node choice works across domains, with the qualitative decision of Figure fig:domains b; bound the effect by the screen on held-out 1 and the concentration on held-out 2.
Node choice works in some domains and not in others (Figure~\ref{fig:domains}b). On the area measure, the model beats random choice on every Elevators, Ferry and Grid task across the three panels. In the held-out Depot decision of Figure~\ref{fig:domains}b, the six open states differ only in where the trucks and crates stand, and the model picks the one that WA* ranks first, without seeing any heuristic value. Leaving out any one domain keeps the gain over random choice between $+0.305$ and $+0.395$ on the third panel and held-out 1, so no single domain carries it, and one round of DAgger did not measurably change choice quality (Appendix~\ref{sec:results-choice-frontier}).

\subsection{Width-Based Traces Hint at Transfer Beyond Planning}
\label{sec:results-transfer}

% Paragraph purpose: Report FOLIO for BFWS vs BFS vs base, GSM8K/HumanEval unchanged, and that the adapters solve no planning task.
Training on BFWS traces raises FOLIO accuracy under all three observation types, whereas BFS traces leave it at the base level. On FOLIO (200 items) the BFWS adapters score 0.660, 0.645 and 0.645 (text, visual, multimodal) against 0.600 for the base, and the BFS adapters score 0.600, 0.605 and 0.605. On GSM8K and HumanEval no adapter moves accuracy by more than 0.024 from the base (0.955 and 0.780). These are the small-set adapters of Section~\ref{sec:results-data-cost}, so the FOLIO gain comes from adapters that solve no planning task. Table~\ref{tab:results-transfer} lists every accuracy. The contrast between the two algorithms is the part worth following up: both adapters saw the same tasks and observations, and only the width-based traces, which rank states by whether they make new facts true, moved the FOLIO score at all. Whether this reflects the novelty computation, the longer operation format, or chance is open.
