\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Paragraph purpose: Background. Planning fine-tuning targets the plan, which records the outcome of search but not its procedure.
The automated planning community has probed the planning ability of large language models by fine-tuning them on planning problems, and the training target is almost always the plan, the action sequence a planner returns, rather than the search that found it \citep{pallagani2022plansformer,rossetti2024learning,bohnet2024exploring,belcamino2026generalization}.
A plan records the outcome of a search but not the procedure behind it: the states the planner considered, the order in which it expanded them, and the ones it set aside.

% Paragraph purpose: Research gap and why it matters now. Search is what makes planners domain-independent; traces target the procedure; prior trace training uses one algorithm (A*); the question extends to VLMs.
Classical planners owe their domain independence to search: one algorithm solves tasks in any PDDL domain, and search algorithms differ mainly in how they order the states still to be expanded.
A search trace records every one of these steps, so training on traces targets the procedure itself.
Prior work has trained models on search traces \citep{lehnert2024beyond,saha2024system1x}, but these studies centre on A* in mazes, Sokoban, and Blocksworld, so how the choice of search algorithm affects what a model learns remains underexplored.
The question extends to vision-language models (VLMs), because recent embodied agents use VLMs to perceive a scene and plan from it end to end \citep{merler2025viplan,xu2025visual,hao2025simulation}, and whether a model can learn to search when the states arrive as images has not been tested in these works.

"Centre on A*" replaces the earlier "single algorithm, not compared" wording, which was wrong for System-1.x (author-approved fix 2026-09-26).

% Paragraph purpose: What we do, without experimental setup. Why these four algorithms (three ways of ordering a search), and why they split into two levels of learning: execution for the rule-ordered searches, search control for the heuristic searches.
We study these questions by fine-tuning a VLM on the search traces of four algorithms that cover three ways of ordering a search.
Breadth-first search (BFS) is blind: it expands states in the order they were generated.
Best-first width search (BFWS) \citep{lipovetzky2017bestfirst} orders states by novelty, preferring states that make a fact or pair of facts true for the first time, and breaks ties by the number of unachieved goals.
Greedy best-first search and weighted A* order states by the additive heuristic $h_{\rm add}$ \citep{bonet2001planning}, an estimate of the distance to the goal, and differ in whether the cost of the path so far also counts.
The model learns from the full trace rather than the plan alone (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}a), at two levels that follow this split.
BFS and BFWS order the search by bookkeeping rules over what has been generated so far, so for them we ask the model to execute the algorithm: the next node to expand is given, and the model writes every step of the search, including where each new node goes in the open list.
The heuristic searches order the search by an estimate of how close a state is to the goal, so for them we ask the model for the search-control decision itself: it chooses which node to expand next, from images of the open nodes and without the heuristic values.
We train and test on 15 PDDL domains, 12 of them shared by every experiment; for execution, each state is shown as text, as an image, or as both, which we call text, visual, and multimodal observations.

% Paragraph purpose: Bridge from setup to findings. Both levels are learnable; preview the two findings that run against expectation.
Our results show that a VLM can learn both levels, and two findings run against a natural expectation: the simplest algorithm, blind BFS, is expensive to learn to execute, and the choice that a heuristic search makes by computing an estimate can be learned from images of the open nodes alone.

% Paragraph purpose: F1. Executing BFS and BFWS is learnable but data-hungry, with text and with images.
Executing BFS and BFWS is learnable, but only with far more data than a small training set provides (Section~\ref{sec:results-data-cost}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}b).
Trained on a small set of traces, the model solves no task under either algorithm in any observation type, and every episode ends on an invalid operation.
With over an order of magnitude more data, it reaches success of 1.0 for BFS and 0.933 for BFWS from text observations, and 1.0 and 0.667 from visual ones.

% Paragraph purpose: F2. Search control for the heuristic searches is learnable from images alone; held-out success vs random; zero-shot at random level.
Choosing the next node for the heuristic searches is learnable from images alone (Section~\ref{sec:results-search-control}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c).
Trained on the choices that greedy best-first search and weighted A* make, and seeing only images of the open nodes, the VLM solves 0.62 and 0.28 of the tasks on two held-out panels within twice the reference budget, against 0.04 and 0.02 for random valid choices.
The untrained VLM chooses at the level of random choices, so the gain comes from the traces.

% Paragraph purpose: Bridge + F3. Transfer of search skills beyond planning is of wide interest; BFWS traces behave differently from BFS traces on FOLIO; hedged as a possible research direction.
Whether search skills learned in one setting carry over to reasoning more generally is a question of wide interest \citep{gandhi2024stream,opedal2026learning}, and here width-based traces behave differently from blind ones.
Training on BFWS traces raises accuracy on the FOLIO logical-reasoning benchmark \citep{han2022folio} from 0.600 to as much as 0.660 (Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}d), whereas training on BFS traces leaves it at 0.600 to 0.605, and neither changes GSM8K or HumanEval accuracy by more than 0.03 \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k,chen2021humaneval}.
This suggests that width-based search teaches a skill that reaches beyond planning, which may be worth pursuing as a research direction in its own right.

% Paragraph purpose: State the three contributions, each a bold lead phrase and one plain sentence, in the vocabulary of the paragraphs above (no implementation terms or raw counts).
We make three contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,parsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{A two-level study of learning search from traces.} We fine-tune a VLM to execute blind and width-based search and to make the search-control decision of heuristic search, over 15 PDDL domains, with states shown as text, as images, or as both.
    \item \textbf{What each level costs.} Executing BFS and BFWS is learnable but needs over an order of magnitude more data than a small training set, while choosing the next node for heuristic search is learned from images alone and beats random valid choice on held-out tasks.
    \item \textbf{What the learned search carries.} Width-based traces point to a reasoning skill that reaches beyond planning.
\end{itemize}
