\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Paragraph purpose: Background. Planning fine-tuning targets the plan, which records the outcome of search but not its procedure; plan-trained models show surface-form sensitivity.
The automated planning community has probed the planning ability of large language models by fine-tuning them on planning problems, and the training target is almost always the plan, the action sequence a planner returns, rather than the search that found it \citep{pallagani2022plansformer,rossetti2024learning,bohnet2024exploring,belcamino2026generalization}.
A plan records the outcome of a search but not the procedure behind it, and there are signs that plan-trained models rely partly on the surface form of their training plans.
\citet{belcamino2026generalization} report that a model trained on 40,000 plans loses significant accuracy when symbols are anonymized or plans are serialized more compactly, although neither change alters what the plans mean.
% Sources: arXiv:2601.14456 abstract (40k IPC-2023 plans; "Symbol anonymization and compact serialization cause significant performance drops despite preserving plan semantics"), checked 2026-09-26. Their unseen-domain result (0%) is deliberately not cited here: we did not test unseen domains.

% Paragraph purpose: Research gap and why it matters now. Search is what makes planners domain-independent; traces target the procedure; prior trace training uses one algorithm (A*); the question extends to VLMs.
Classical planners owe their domain independence to search: one algorithm solves tasks in any PDDL domain, and search algorithms differ mainly in how they order the states still to be expanded.
A search trace records every one of these steps, so training on traces targets the procedure itself.
Prior work has trained models on search traces \citep{lehnert2024beyond,saha2024system1x}, but these studies centre on A* in mazes, Sokoban, and Blocksworld, so how the choice of search algorithm affects what a model learns remains underexplored.
The question extends to vision-language models (VLMs), because recent embodied agents use VLMs to perceive a scene and plan from it end to end \citep{merler2025viplan,xu2025visual,hao2025simulation}, and how the observation modality affects learning to search is unknown.
% Sources: trace-training credits per Section 2 ¶2 (Searchformer: A* in mazes and Sokoban, arXiv:2402.14083 abstract; System-1.x: A* main experiments in mazes and Blocksworld, BFS/DFS only as a maze appendix study showing its planner works with either, arXiv:2407.14414 Sec. 4.3 and App. B Fig. 6). "Centre on A*" replaces the earlier "single algorithm, not compared" wording, which was wrong for System-1.x (author-approved fix 2026-09-26).

% Paragraph purpose: What we do, without experimental setup. Why these four algorithms (three ways of ordering a search), that the model executes the search and learns from the full trace, and the three observation types.
We study these questions by fine-tuning a VLM on the search traces of four algorithms that cover three ways of ordering a search.
Breadth-first search (BFS) is blind: it expands states in the order they were generated.
Best-first width search (BFWS) \citep{lipovetzky2017bestfirst} orders states by novelty, preferring states that make a fact or pair of facts true for the first time, and breaks ties by the number of unachieved goals.
Greedy best-first search and weighted A* order states by the additive heuristic $h_{\rm add}$ \citep{bonet2001planning}, an estimate of the distance to the goal, and differ in whether the cost of the path so far also counts.
The model executes the search itself, one operation at a time, and learns from the full trace rather than the plan alone (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}a).
We train and test it on 12 PDDL domains, with each state shown as text, as an image, or as both, which we call text, visual, and multimodal observations.

BFWS is goal-aware (goal-count tie-break) and unpruned, so it is not described as blind or as pruning.
% Evidence (12 domains, three observation types): docs/experiments/expanded-study/baseline-protocol.json (algorithms, modalities); synthesis-v1/README.md §2.

% Paragraph purpose: Bridge from setup to findings. Announce that every algorithm is learnable and preview the two counter-intuitive findings (BFS most expensive; images contribute little).
Our results show that a VLM can learn to execute every one of these algorithms, and two findings run against a natural expectation: the simplest algorithm, blind BFS, is among the most expensive to learn, and the images a VLM receives contribute little to what it learns.
% Scope: "images contribute little" rests on the corruption study of the additive adapters (next paragraphs and Section 5.2).

% Paragraph purpose: F1. Data cost depends on the algorithm; success rates, not raw counts.
The data cost of learning depends strongly on the algorithm (Section~\ref{sec:results-data-cost}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}b).
Weighted A* and greedy best-first search reach success rates of 0.833 and 0.903 from a small training set, whereas BFS and BFWS remain at 0 and reach 1.0 and 0.933 only with over an order of magnitude more data.

% Paragraph purpose: F2. The learned search relies on the text rather than the images; multimodal contrast only.
The learned search relies on the text rather than the images (Section~\ref{sec:results-modality}, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}c).
When the model sees both, shuffling the text lowers its success by 0.722, whereas blanking or degrading the image lowers it by at most 0.111.
% Evidence: outputs/expanded-study/v1/modality-stress/analysis.json contrasts.degradation_by_family_modality (text-shuffled__multimodal-state -0.722 [-0.889, -0.500]; visual-blank__multimodal-state and visual-degraded__multimodal-state -0.111 [-0.278, 0.000]). Scope (stated in Section 5.2): corruption study on the additive adapters, 9 tasks x 2 algorithms; the candidate list is text in every observation type.

% Paragraph purpose: F3. The learned heuristic search is insensitive to surface representations, where plan-trained models are reported to be sensitive (Belcamino's phrasing); also holds on larger tasks.
The learned heuristic search is also insensitive to the surface representation of a task (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}).
Plan-trained models have been reported to show strong sensitivity to surface representations, losing significant accuracy under symbol anonymization that preserves plan semantics \citep{belcamino2026generalization}, but our trace-trained models show no such drop.
Their success stays at 0.967 under object renaming and 0.900 under name compression and image restyling, holds at 0.867 on scaled-up tasks, and falls to 0.633 only when the initial state is shifted.
% Evidence: docs/experiments/expanded-study/synthesis-v1/README.md §6 robustness table (object-renaming 29/30, name-compression 27/30, render-restyle 27/30, scale-up 26/30, shifted-init 19/30; learned 128/150, base 0/150, random_valid 750/750, exact_reference 150/150). Scope (stated in Section 5.3): additive adapters only; no plan-only baseline.
% Sources: arXiv:2601.14456 abstract ("instance-wise symbol anonymization"; "significant performance drops despite preserving plan semantics, thus revealing strong sensitivity to surface representations"), checked 2026-09-26.

% Paragraph purpose: Bridge + F4. Transfer of search skills beyond planning is of wide interest; BFWS traces behave differently from the other algorithms on FOLIO; hedged as a possible research direction.
Whether search skills learned in one setting carry over to reasoning more generally is a question of wide interest \citep{gandhi2024stream,opedal2026learning}, and here width-based traces behave differently from the rest.
Training on BFWS traces raises accuracy on the FOLIO logical-reasoning benchmark \citep{han2022folio} from 0.600 to as much as 0.660 (Section~\ref{sec:results-transfer}), whereas training on the other three algorithms leaves it at or below 0.605, and no trained model changes GSM8K or HumanEval accuracy by more than 0.04 \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k,chen2021humaneval}.
This suggests that width-based search teaches a skill that reaches beyond planning, which may be worth pursuing as a research direction in its own right.

% Paragraph purpose: State the three contributions, each a bold lead phrase and one plain sentence, in the vocabulary of the paragraphs above (no implementation terms or raw counts).
We make three contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,parsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{A study of learning search from traces.} We fine-tune a VLM on the traces of blind, width-based, and heuristic search over 12 PDDL domains, with text, visual, and multimodal observations.
    \item \textbf{What learning depends on.} The data cost of learning a search algorithm varies by over an order of magnitude across algorithms, and the learned search relies on the text rather than the images.
    \item \textbf{What the learned search carries.} Trace-trained heuristic search is insensitive to the surface representation of a task, and width-based traces point to a reasoning skill that reaches beyond planning.
\end{itemize}
