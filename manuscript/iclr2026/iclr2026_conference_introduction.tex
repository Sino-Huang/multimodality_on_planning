\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Paragraph purpose: State why search choice needs its own measurement, the policy/runtime mechanism and scope, and the natural control.
A learned search policy is useful only if it chooses well among valid operations, yet its success can also reflect validity alone.
On classical planning probes \citep{valmeekam2023critical,stein2025autoplanbench,kokel2026acpbenchhard}, off-the-shelf language models improve at operational reasoning but not at structural enumeration \citep{huang2026twodistinct}.
We train a Search Process Policy to emit Typed Search Operations, which a no-repair Trusted Search Runtime validates, under text, visual, and multimodal observations (Section~\ref{sec:policy}).
The declared algorithms are breadth-first search (BFS), width-based search, and additive best-first search, which is best-first search ordered by the additive heuristic $h_{\rm add}$.
The natural control is random-valid, uniform over valid operations, but without a check a benchmark can credit validity as choice.

In the enumeration contract's additive best-first cells, random-valid makes exactly the decisions of the exact reference, the declared algorithm run as specified.
There each expansion submits its complete candidate set and ties break in sorted order, and the two agree on 48/48 pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:contracts}a).

Breaking ties in submission order gives 19/48 divergent pairs, so the identity audit, a decision-by-decision comparison of the two, can return either verdict.

% Paragraph purpose: State the thesis as two checks, then the choice-frontier contract, M1, and the ladder that validates it, with the privileged-rung boundary.
We argue that random-valid success measures choice only if the identity audit finds divergent decisions and the budget check finds that the success budget binds decisions, limiting how many decisions a chooser can make whatever tokens it spends.
We build a choice-frontier contract, where the policy picks the runtime-owned frontier state to expand under a budget of twice the reference's expansion count (Figure~\ref{fig:contracts}b).
M1, the area under the solve-versus-budget curve, is first validated by a pre-registered ladder of exact-$\epsilon$ selectors, which randomize a fraction $\epsilon$ of reference decisions.
On the validation panel, 12 fresh tasks from 7 domains, the ladder passes.
% Evidence: configs/experiments/choice-frontier-v2/membership.json task_ids (12), domains (7); outputs/choice-frontier/v2/metrics/analysis.json instrument_validity.verdict PASS (#135 E135-01..16).
Every adjacent gap is positive (smallest $+0.082$ $[+0.051, +0.111]$, Section~\ref{sec:results-ladder}).
% Evidence: outputs/choice-frontier/v2/metrics/analysis.json instrument_validity.pairs[0].difference and .ci95 (#135 E135-05, E135-06).
The rungs read the reference's computed priority order, which is never shown to a policy, so the ladder validates the measurement, not a policy.

% Paragraph purpose: State the four contributions without numbers; receipts are in the paragraphs above.
This paper makes four contributions.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,parsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{The identity audit, a per-decision check that returns both verdicts on one runtime.}
    \item \textbf{A choice-frontier measurement validated by a pre-registered exact-$\epsilon$ ladder before any policy is scored.}
    \item \textbf{A trained adapter above random-valid on three panels, with its ladder position and screening boundary.}
    \item \textbf{An audit of two published interfaces that applies the compute-matched-baseline principle \citep{li2019random} as a budget check, with random-valid registering choice on one and saturating by construction on the other.}
\end{itemize}
