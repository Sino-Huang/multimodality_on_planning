## Findings

- **CRITICAL — the paper's central empirical question remains unanswered by its only completed comparison.** The Introduction asks whether learned execution “**improves structural/process competence beyond the valid but uninformed exploration of a random-valid reference**” (`iclr2026_conference.tex:75`), but immediately reports that “**the random-valid reference also solved every task**” and there is “**no learned advantage**” (`:78`; also Abstract `:57`). The contribution consequently offers a “**bounded first result**” (`:86`), but that result cannot establish the claimed intervention's value rather than a panel on which valid random exploration suffices. BFWS—the stated structural route that might discriminate the hypothesis—has “**training and adjudication remain unfinished**” (`:78`). The prose no longer overclaims; the fatal problem is that the actual evidence is still an inconclusive feasibility result.

- **MINOR — the new opening repeats its controlled-probe justification rather than advancing it.** The first sentence already says instances can be “**generated systematically, solutions verified automatically, and difficulty controlled precisely**,” while the last says the same properties “**let an evaluator vary difficulty along controlled dimensions and check each answer mechanically**” (`:63`). This is redundant in a four-sentence opening and weakens the otherwise sharper progression. Remove or replace the final restatement with the next conceptual move.

## Verdict / next recommendation

**REJECT.** The rewrite is materially clearer, less repetitive overall, and correctly separates observed BFS evidence from prospective work. Its single most damaging issue is unchanged: zero gain against random-valid means it does not support the intervention's central claim. Complete the discriminative BFWS comparison before presenting this as evidence that training yields structural/process competence beyond uninformed valid exploration.
